I checked every superintendent change in every Massachusetts school district since 2014: 336 districts, about 640 changes, traced through DESE data, town minutes and news reports. Appointing an insider without an open search is common; about one permanent hire in six statewide. Naming the next superintendent at the same meeting where the departure is announced, with no search and no interim, is not: I could only find four cases in twelve years. Among the 39 districts the state or geography compares Sharon to, there are none.

In full transparency, these are the stories of the four cases

Hudson, March 25, 2014. With four of seven seats voting, the committee named its assistant superintendent of two years the night the superintendent said he was leaving. Two weeks later she asked the committee to reopen: "An outside partner would help to insure the process is as open and transparent as possible." The committee brought in the state association of school committees, ran nine public forums and a public interview, and re-voted 5-1-1 in June. Her entry plan was in front of the committee by September, with a findings report due in January. Four committee members, including the chair, resigned in the ten months around the vote. She left in 2016 at the end of her first contract, and the district hired a search firm. The next chair on the first vote: "That is not something that I want to see replicated, the speed at which it happened."

Silver Lake (Kingston, Halifax, Plympton), December 2014. The assistant superintendent of nine years was named superintendent-elect the night the superintendent announced his June retirement; the minutes show no vote. Both committees approved her three-year contract within three months. The outgoing superintendent stayed to June 30 for the handover; her entry plan was in front of the committee by September, her goals by January, and her first evaluation was approved in open session that April. The post she vacated was advertised, drew 54 applicants, and finalists were interviewed at a joint meeting. When she retired, the district hired a search firm rather than, in one chair's words, "trying to freehand it ourselves".

Wakefield, June 9, 2015. Eight days after the state named the superintendent Holyoke's receiver, the committee accepted her resignation and appointed the assistant superintendent by "a direct appointment without any need for a search or other processes", unanimously. The appointee had spent thirty years in the district and, two years earlier, had been one of four finalists in a consultant-run search with public site visits, losing 4-3; the committee's stated reason was that she had been "vetted through that same process". Her contract was settled in fourteen days. She held public office hours through August and presented her core values to the committee in September. She retired in 2018, and her successor came from a public interview of three finalists.

Richmond, April 25, 2023. A one-school town of about 150 students restructured its shared-services agreement so its part-time superintendent, who runs a neighbouring regional district, stepped back to an on-call role, and made the principal principal-superintendent. Both votes were unanimous roll calls; her three-year contract, with the evaluation process written into it, passed the same night. She had been principal for one year and a building leader for seventeen. Her combined goals were adopted in September with monthly progress reports.

None of these look like Sharon. Two had a competitive vetting to point to, one had nine years in the deputy's chair, and one was a one-school town merging two jobs into one.

Most districts that promote an insider do fine, and the insider stays longer than a searched outsider. But in nearly every one of those cases the insider was still put through something: a posted internal search, a public interview, community forums, or a year as interim before the permanent vote. Of the eight interims later made permanent without an open search in Sharon's peer group, three were interviewed or surveyed in public before the permanent vote, four had been interviewed publicly when they were chosen as interim, and one (Hingham) had no public step on record. The failures cluster where the process was thinnest.

No posting, one name, objection overruled

  • Chicopee, 2019. The assistant superintendent was promoted at a special meeting seven days after the departure became public, with no posting; the mayor objected at the vote: "I'm looking for this position to be posted." Arrested in 2022 for lying to the FBI, placed on leave 8-3, pleaded guilty in 2024; the replacement search deadlocked.
  • Auburn, 2020. The committee voted to run an internal-only search; it drew one applicant, the high school principal. Resigned in 2022 during a university probe into whether he fabricated parts of his dissertation. The committee named his successor thirteen days later, again without a search.
  • Southbridge, 2014. The committee said it was "too late to search" and appointed its director of teaching and learning 4-0-1. Employee complaints, a lawyer hired to investigate, resignation at an emergency meeting six months in. The next hire was also cut short of its posted search and resigned within a month. The state took the district into receivership in January 2016.
  • Stoneham, 2022. Promoted from within "over a hiring search or an interim tag". After a 2024 safety incident a 500-signature petition argued he "was not chosen for his current position based on a pool of qualified applicants". The committee kept him; he left the next year.

Interim made permanent with no search, no interview, or no agenda item

  • Milton, 2021. The acting superintendent was made permanent by a vote that was not on the agenda; the chair: "We felt there wasn't a need for a search because we already had the best person." A member: "I didn't expect us to take a vote that night." The local paper filed an Open Meeting Law complaint. He was arrested in 2022, placed on leave, and resigned at 22 months.
  • Lynnfield, 2024. Interim named the night the departure was announced; made permanent four and a half months later with no search committee and no interview. A member at the vote: "It's mind boggling to me that we would hire someone to be one of the highest paid leaders in town without so much as an interview." Contract extension passed 3-2, a resident petition for a search, a packed meeting and a state complaint followed. He is still in post; the dispute is about legitimacy, not results.
  • Andover, 2015. Interim converted to a four-year contract after three months; the chair: "We would lose Berman if we started searching for candidates now." Parents called it "hasty"; the paper asked "What was the rush?" A parent petition in 2018, a union no-confidence vote in 2020, resignation that December.
  • Uxbridge, 2023. Interim converted with no permanent posting. Six of seven committee members resigned within the year; an outside report cited his refusal to take part in his own evaluation; a federal suit settled for $60,000; leave after an arrest and a buyout in 2025.
  • Holliston, 2020. The chair promised a search "during the SY2020-2021"; the interim was made permanent by contract addendum in December instead. Teachers voted no confidence 303 to 7 in 2023, citing 216 new staff in two and a half years.

Where we are, two months in

No contract has been negotiated, voted or signed. No references were called; the chair described the outgoing superintendent's endorsement as "a reference check of sorts". The one public session with the appointee came fourteen days after the vote, with members told to "refrain from asking vetting questions". A motion to rescind failed 2-4. A transition subcommittee failed 2-4. The committee's annual goals, adopted September 16, contain no superintendent evaluation or transition item. Three of the five members who voted yes have said on the record that the process was rushed, that the committee "failed the community", or that the interview "should have" come before the decision.

Sources: DESE staffing retention and district profile data (report years 2014 to 2026), school committee minutes and recordings, and local press, for every non-charter district. Receivership districts are excluded from the same-meeting count. Quotations are from minutes, meeting recordings or the cited newspaper. Method note: a "same-meeting" appointment means the permanent successor was named at the meeting where the departure was announced or accepted, with no posting, no other candidate and no interim.
